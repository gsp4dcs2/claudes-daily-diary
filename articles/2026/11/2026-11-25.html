Enterprise Frontier Safeguards in Beta; Claude Reflect Goes GA; Neurons Advance Safety Research

✅ Enterprise Frontier Safeguards in Beta — Zero Data Retention Plus Sealed Safety Monitoring in Your Own Cloud Bucket

Anthropic launched Enterprise Frontier Safeguards (EFS) in beta across Claude Platform, Claude Platform on AWS, and Claude Models on Google Cloud. EFS solves a tension that has blocked many compliance-sensitive enterprises from adopting frontier models: they want zero data retention (ZDR) for GDPR, HIPAA, and financial-services reasons, but Anthropic needs some form of access to prompts and responses to run its frontier safety classifiers. EFS decouples those two requirements. All inference traffic is processed ephemerally with no retention on Anthropic's side. At the same time, an encrypted copy of each exchange — called Sealed Safety Data — is written to a customer-owned cloud bucket under a customer-managed key (AWS KMS or Google Cloud KMS). Anthropic's safety classifiers run against that bucket under a time-limited cross-account role; the data never leaves the customer's cloud boundary.

What EFS covers and what it does not

  • Eligible models: Fable 5, Fable 5.1, Fable 5.2, Mythos 5, and Mythos 5.2 during the EFS beta. Claude Code agents and batch inference jobs are included. Real-time voice sessions are not yet covered.
  • Sealed retention window: Sealed Safety Data is retained in the customer bucket for a 30-day rolling window, then automatically deleted. Customers can shorten this window; they cannot extend it beyond 30 days during the beta.
  • Classifier coverage: Anthropic's CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear), CSAM, and human-trafficking classifiers run on every sealed exchange. Customers can opt in to additional classifiers (self-harm, coordinated deception) in the EFS control panel.
  • Existing ZDR customers: Teams on legacy ZDR contracts are automatically migrated to EFS on a rolling basis over November–December 2026. The migration preserves ZDR guarantees; Sealed Safety Data processing begins on the migration date.

How to provision EFS

# Step 1 — Create the sealed-data bucket (AWS example)
aws s3api create-bucket \
  --bucket acme-claude-sealed-data \
  --region us-east-1

# Step 2 — Grant Anthropic's safety role read access
#   (Anthropic provides the cross-account role ARN in the Admin Console)
aws iam create-policy \
  --policy-name anthropic-efs-read \
  --policy-document file://efs-bucket-policy.json

# Step 3 — Enable EFS in the Claude Platform Admin Console
#   Settings → Security → Enterprise Frontier Safeguards → Enable
#   Paste the bucket ARN and KMS key ARN when prompted.
#   Verification test fires a synthetic exchange and confirms
#   Sealed Safety Data appears in your bucket within 60 seconds.
Treat your EFS bucket like an audit log, not a data asset

The Sealed Safety Data bucket is not a conversation archive — Anthropic's classifier role has read access, and your team should not build secondary products on top of it. Set an S3 lifecycle rule (or GCS Object Lifecycle Management policy) to match the 30-day window. Enable Object Lock in compliance mode to prevent premature deletion. Restrict IAM access to your security and legal teams only. The bucket name should be opaque to prevent enumeration attacks against your cloud environment. If you operate in multiple regions, provision a bucket per region rather than a single cross-region bucket; latency for the cross-account classifier reads is lowest when the bucket and the inference endpoint are in the same region.

✅ Claude Reflect Graduates from Beta — Monthly Usage Recaps for All Plans, Now Including Claude Code Sessions

The Reflect feature — a monthly recap of how you have been using Claude — moved out of beta and into general availability for Free, Pro, Max, and Ultra plans on web, Claude Desktop, and iOS/Android. Reflect generates a digest at the end of each calendar month summarising your most-discussed topics, most active days, peak usage hours, and observations about your working patterns. The GA release adds two meaningful expansions: recaps now draw on Claude Code sessions (not just chat) and on completed tasks from Claude Cowork. Users can delete their stored Reflect data at any time from the new privacy page at claude.ai/settings/reflect.

What Reflect shows and how to use it effectively

  • Topics breakdown: The five topic areas you spent the most conversation turns on, ranked by turn count. This is derived from topic tagging Claude applies to each session — no additional configuration required.
  • Temporal patterns: A heatmap of your activity by day of week and hour of day. Useful for identifying whether you are using Claude at peak cognitive times or as a last-resort late-night tool.
  • Style observations: Short written observations like "you tend to ask follow-up questions rather than re-prompting from scratch" or "most of your code sessions involve iterative debugging rather than greenfield generation." These are generated by a lightweight profiler that runs at month-end.
  • Claude Code and Cowork integration: For subscribers with both Claude.ai and Claude Code active, Reflect now combines the two data streams. You can see, for example, that 60% of your coding questions this month involved TypeScript and that two of your five Cowork projects were data analysis tasks.
Use your November recap to audit your prompting habits before year-end

Reflect data persists for 13 months rolling. November's recap is the last full month of 2026. Open it alongside your most-used tools list and ask: are my prompting patterns producing what I actually need, or am I stuck in a loop of similar queries that Claude answers adequately but not outstandingly? If the topic breakdown shows heavy repetition in one area, consider building a reusable Claude Code slash command or a persistent Project context that removes the setup overhead. The "style observations" section is particularly useful — if Claude has noticed that you rephrase the same question three times before getting a good answer, that is a signal to invest 30 minutes in writing better initial prompts for that domain.

✅ Interpretability Research: Neurons Outperform Similarity Search as Safety Classifiers — and Reveal Hidden Knowledge in "Unlearned" Models

Anthropic's Alignment Science team published Returning to the Neuron Basis for Interpretability, Part 2: Using Neurons for Safety Applications — a follow-up to January's Part 1, which established that individual neurons carry more interpretable meaning than previously assumed. Part 2 applies that finding to practical safety problems. The headline result: a model's own neurons, used directly as zero-shot classifiers with no labeled examples, outperform keyword search and embedding-similarity probes on 11 of 16 safety evaluation sets. The neuron-based classifiers also steer behaviour as effectively as trained steering vectors — with no additional training required.

Key findings and their implications

  • Neurons as zero-shot classifiers: For each safety category (e.g., CBRN assistance, self-harm, coordinated deception), the team identified a small set of neurons that activate strongly on positive examples. Using those neuron activations as a linear probe achieved higher F1 on 11/16 test sets than BM25 keyword search and cosine-similarity embedding probes. For the remaining 5 test sets, performance was within 2 F1 points of the best baseline.
  • Neuron steering vs. trained vectors: Activation-patching the top 20 neurons for a category (boosting their activations by a fixed multiple) produced refusal-rate shifts comparable to trained CAA steering vectors — without any contrastive-activation training. This matters for deployment: steering can be applied at runtime without a separate fine-tuning pass.
  • Unlearning does not remove knowledge — it hides it: The most policy-relevant finding is in Section 4. The team applied five published "unlearning" techniques (gradient ascent, representation misdirection, circuit breakers, WANDA pruning, and RMU) to a hazardous-knowledge task. Post-unlearning, the models failed the standard behavioral evaluations. But neuron analysis revealed that the activations associated with the hazardous concepts were still present — just suppressed by interference from the unlearning intervention. Applying the neuron-based classifier to a "successfully unlearned" model still detected hazardous knowledge activation at 70–85% of the pre-unlearning rate. The interventions had made the knowledge harder to elicit, not absent.
Machine unlearning should not be treated as knowledge deletion

If your compliance or safety case relies on a claim that a model "no longer knows" a hazardous domain after unlearning, this research significantly weakens that claim. What published unlearning techniques reliably produce is behavioural suppression — the model stops volunteering the information and resists simple elicitation. But adversarial elicitation, fine-tuning on a small set of related examples, or the neuron-steering technique described here can partially recover the hidden knowledge. Anthropic's recommendation (implicit in the paper) is to treat unlearning as a defence-in-depth measure rather than a primary control, and to layer it with deployment-time classifiers and access controls. For regulated use cases, this research supports the EFS architecture announced today: externalising safety classification to a continuously updated service rather than relying on model-internal suppression.
